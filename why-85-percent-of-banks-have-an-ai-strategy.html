Why 85% of Banks Have an AI Strategy and 12% Have Something in Production

73 percentage points. That's not a gap. That's an industry lying to itself.

A few weeks ago I was at a dinner in Milan with six people who run technology at major European banks. Not a conference. Not a panel. A dinner. Wine, pasta, honest conversation.

At some point someone asked the table: "Who here has AI in production?" And by production they didn't mean a chatbot on the customer service page. They meant AI that's integrated into a core business workflow, touching real data, making or supporting real decisions, every day.

Two hands went up. Out of six. And one of those two said: "Barely."

These are sophisticated institutions. Each one has an AI strategy that's been presented to the board. Each one has a budget line for it. Each one can point to pilots, partnerships, proofs of concept. And yet most of them are nowhere close to having AI do actual work in their organizations.

That dinner is the banking industry in miniature.

The number everyone quotes and nobody questions

85% of banks have an AI strategy. You've seen this number, or something close to it, in every consulting report published in the last two years. It sounds impressive. It suggests an industry in motion.

Then you look at the other number. Somewhere between 10% and 15% of those banks have AI running in a production environment. Not piloting. Not testing. Not "evaluating." Actually running.

That's a 73-point gap between saying and doing. And I don't think most people in the industry appreciate how extraordinary that number is.

In any other sector, if 85% of companies said they had a strategy for something and only 12% had executed it, we'd call it a crisis of execution. In banking, we call it "progress." We celebrate the 85% as evidence that the industry is taking AI seriously, and we quietly ignore the 12% that tells you almost nobody has figured out how to make it work.

I spend my weeks in rooms with the people behind both numbers. The ones with the strategy deck. The ones with the production deployment. And I can tell you: they live on different planets.

What an AI strategy actually looks like inside a bank

Let me describe a real AI strategy. I won't name the bank, but if you work in European banking you'll recognize the pattern because it's everywhere.

There's a 90-page document. It was produced by a combination of the CIO's office, an external consultancy, and the newly created "AI Center of Excellence." It covers: the strategic rationale for AI adoption, a maturity assessment of the bank's data infrastructure, a risk framework, a governance model, a list of 20+ potential use cases ranked by feasibility and impact, a three-year roadmap with milestones, and a budget request.

It's thorough. It's well-written. It took six months to produce. The board approved it with enthusiasm.

And it has absolutely no relationship to what's actually happening on the ground.

The 20 use cases? Three got funded. Of those three, one is stuck in procurement, one is in a sandbox with synthetic data, and one was quietly shelved because the business line that was supposed to use it never agreed to change their process. The three-year roadmap? It was obsolete within four months because the technology moved faster than the document's assumptions. The governance model? Nobody can explain who actually has the authority to approve a production deployment.

The strategy exists. Execution doesn't. And the strategy's existence makes everyone feel like something is happening, which is worse than having no strategy at all. Because at least then you'd feel the urgency.

The meetings that never happen

Here's what I've noticed after sitting in hundreds of these conversations. The AI strategy is created by one group of people. The decision to put AI into production would need to be made by a completely different group of people. And those two groups almost never sit in the same room.

The strategy is built by the CIO, the CDO, the innovation team, maybe some external advisors. These are smart people. They understand the technology. They can articulate the opportunity.

But the person who decides whether the wealth management division actually adopts an AI tool for client preparation? That's the Head of Wealth Management. They have their own P&L, their own priorities, their own problems. They weren't in the strategy meetings. They didn't write the document. And they have zero incentive to take on the risk of deploying something new when their current numbers are fine.

I had a conversation in Zurich that made this painfully clear. The CDO of a private bank told me, with visible frustration: "I've been trying to get the advisory team to use our AI tool for six months. It works. It saves time. They won't touch it. Their head says it's not a priority this quarter."

The CDO had the strategy. The Head of Advisory had the power. They wanted different things. Nobody above them was forcing alignment.

This is the gap. It's not technical. It's organizational. The strategy lives in one part of the org chart. The authority to execute lives in another. And the bridge between them doesn't exist.

Why the consultants can't fix this

I should be careful here because I spent years at Deloitte and I respect the work. But I'll say what I've come to believe after seeing this from the other side.

The consulting model is excellent at producing strategies. That's the product. A strategy engagement runs for three to six months, involves workshops and assessments and benchmarks, and delivers a beautiful document with a roadmap. The partner presents it to the board. Everyone agrees it's the right direction. The invoice gets paid.

Then the consulting team leaves. And the bank is left with a document and no mechanism to turn it into reality.

I'm not saying this is anyone's fault, exactly. The consultancy was hired to build a strategy. They built one. But the implicit promise — that having a strategy would lead to execution — is where things fall apart. Because execution requires sustained operational effort over 12 to 24 months. It requires someone inside the bank, with authority and a mandate, to push through the procurement, the integration, the change management, the compliance review, the training. That's not a strategy project. That's a transformation. And transformations are messier, slower, and less prestigious than strategy work.

So the bank has a strategy. The board feels good. And the actual work of putting AI into production hasn't started.

The 12% and what they did differently

I pay very close attention to the banks that have actually shipped. They're a small group, but they're not random. There's a pattern.

They all had a senior executive who owned the outcome, not the strategy. Not "we're exploring AI" but "I need this specific workflow automated by September and my bonus depends on it." When there's personal accountability at the C-level for a specific production deployment, things move. When there's collective responsibility for a vague "AI transformation," nothing moves.

They all started with an embarrassingly narrow scope. Not "transform the advisory experience." Instead: "Cut the time it takes an advisor to prepare for a client meeting from 45 minutes to 10." One use case. One user. One metric. You can't hide behind a slide deck when the goal is that specific. Either the advisor's prep time dropped or it didn't.

They all involved compliance from day one. I've written about this before and I'll keep saying it because banks keep getting it wrong. If compliance reviews your AI tool after it's built, you'll spend six months in remediation. If compliance is in the room while you're building it, you design around the constraints from the start. The EU AI Act actually helps here — it gives everyone a shared framework instead of each compliance officer inventing their own.

And they all had a hard deadline. Not a roadmap milestone. A date. A real one. With consequences. One bank I work with set a 90-day window: build, test, ship, or kill. No extensions. No "phase two evaluations." That constraint forced every department — IT, compliance, business, operations — to actually coordinate because there was no time to play territory games.

The strategy deck is the problem

I'm going to say something that will annoy a lot of people who spent six months building AI strategy documents: the strategy deck itself is part of the problem.

Not because strategy is unimportant. But because the act of creating a comprehensive strategy gives the organization a false sense of progress. We did the strategy. Check. Now we're "on the journey." The language of progress — "we're ramping up," "we're building capabilities," "we're evaluating partners" — becomes a substitute for the thing itself.

I've sat in board meetings where the AI update was a 20-minute presentation full of activity metrics. Number of pilots running. Number of use cases identified. Number of vendor conversations. Number of employees trained in "AI literacy." All of it looked like progress. None of it was progress. Because the only metric that matters is: is AI doing real work in this bank, today?

The banks in the 12% don't have better strategies. Some of them don't have a formal AI strategy at all. What they have is a specific problem, a person accountable for solving it, and enough organizational authority to get it done. The rest is noise.

73 points of organizational debt

The 73-point gap between strategy and production isn't a technology problem. AI works. I demonstrate it every week. The models are good enough. The integrations are solvable. The compliance frameworks exist.

The gap is organizational debt. Decades of it. Siloed departments that don't share data or priorities. Procurement processes designed for hardware purchases in 2005. Risk frameworks that can't evaluate probabilistic systems. Incentive structures that reward activity over outcomes. And a culture, deeply embedded in European banking, that equates caution with competence.

None of this is easy to fix. I'm not pretending it is. But the first step is to stop pretending that the 85% number means something. It doesn't. Having an AI strategy in 2026 means nothing. Every bank has one. It's table stakes.

The question that matters is the uncomfortable one: is your AI doing work today that a human was doing yesterday? If the answer is no, you don't have an AI capability. You have a document.

And a document is not a moat. It's not even a start. It's a comforting story the organization tells itself while the banks in the 12% are building something real.
